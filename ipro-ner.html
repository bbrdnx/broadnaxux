Overview

Making data navigable, not just searchable

IPRO brought machine learning into legal review with named entity recognition: NLP models that read across a repository and automatically surface the people, organizations, money, dates, and sensitive PII buried in millions of documents. The bet was that reviewers find more, faster, when they can see the entities and conversations behind a case instead of guessing at keywords.

My job was to turn that AI capability into an experience non-technical reviewers could trust. I designed using Material UI to keep engineering moving, partnered with NexLP on what their entity API could and couldn't do, and worked alongside IPRO's data science team as they evaluated the underlying NER models so my design decisions matched what the models could actually deliver.

The AI Foundation

Designing on top of live machine learning

This was not AI as a buzzword. The product ran on real entity-recognition models, and I sat in the evaluations where the data science team benchmarked IPRO's in-house NER models against the commercial baselines: AWS and Azure, each split into separate PII and PHI services. Understanding those results directly shaped what I designed.

The takeaway was sobering and useful: the models were fast, but recall and accuracy were limited across the board, and the most sensitive entity types (social security numbers, credit cards, passports) were both the hardest to detect reliably and the most consequential to miss. False positives were a real risk on broad types like person and address.

So I designed for a human-in-the-loop reality instead of treating model output as truth. Entity recognition narrows the haystack; people make the calls. That principle drove concrete decisions: confirmable redaction rather than blind automation, document and custodian holds, partial redaction that masks the first digits of a card while keeping the last four for identification, and explicit warnings before any irreversible action.

Research

Testing with expert users

I ran concept testing with five expert users (a mix of internal service providers and external clients) in 45-minute prototype walkthroughs. The pattern was clear: NexLP had power, but the surface was punishing.

  • Tooltips too sparse. Users guessing.
  • Actions scattered across the screen, no logic to placement.
  • Merging complex, with no undo.
  • Colors and symbols, no legend.
  • Profile cards looked tappable, weren't.

Concept Design

Five integrated components

I designed five features that translated API capabilities into a repeatable flow for early data analysis (EDA).

  • A full-width communication chart with a collapsible entities panel, sized to actually be legible, with drill-down via the API.
  • An entities launchpad wired into search.
  • Person profiles that surface details only at the moment of need.
  • A merge dialogue with explicit warnings (no undo on the back end).
  • NexLP's chart controls, layered with the tooltips, legends, and help text the original lacked.

NER in Action

One investigation, no keywords

To make the AI concrete for stakeholders, I designed and presented an end-to-end scenario: a suspected donation-fraud investigation, starting from almost nothing. The only leads were a $150 charge on a card ending 4637 and a charity name. No keyword list.

The entire path ran through machine-detected entities. Filter the Money entity to $150, narrow by date, and a deleted email surfaces with a matching receipt. A partial signer name leads through the People entities to a single suspect, whose profile (email addresses, concepts, and frequent communicators) reveals he was collecting donations through Venmo. A chat thread then exposes a donor's full card number and expiration, sent in plain text. From there, I selected the exposed PII, created a redaction category, and pushed it straight into Search & Redact.

The scenario showed what entity recognition unlocks: money, dates, people, organizations, PII, and conversational concepts, detected automatically, turning a million-document repository into a few decisive clicks. It also grounded the abstraction in a real risk I cited, where a government agency released thousands of pages in a public records response and tried to claw redactions back a day later, after the data was already out.

Findings

What users valued most

4.13

Average experience rating (out of 5)

5/5

Users engaged with chart interactivity

The entity list was the runaway favorite. All five users said it would change how they built searches. Every participant engaged with chart interactivity, including features I wasn't sure would land. They also expected the chart to update in tandem like a live search, an ambition I scoped into a later phase.

One thing I cut: the business card section of entity profiles. Users could see it, but couldn't tell me what they'd do with it. I recommended dropping it.

Recommendations

Phased rollout: now, next, later

I structured implementation in three phases so engineering could ship value without waiting for the full vision.

  • Now: NexLP API for chart, controls, and entities list. Plus quick wins (entities visible by default, max chart container space).
  • Next: tighter integration. Better merge visibility, refreshed profile cards from user feedback, timeline integration, support for live Early Data Assessment.
  • Later: entities across other widgets, NexLP's tree chart, full-size chart views, and the live-search behavior users had asked for.

Vision in long shots, ship in short ones.
