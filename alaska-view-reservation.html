Alaska Airlines

View Reservation

Modernizing the most-visited post-booking page at Alaska Airlines, on top of a decade-old monolith.

About Alaska Airlines

Alaska Airlines is a Seattle-based carrier with roughly 2,000 daily departures and the largest presence at SEA. Founded in 1932, Alaska grew up serving the Pacific Northwest, then went global in 2024 with the acquisition of Hawaiian Airlines.

Role

Lead Product Designer

Platform

Mobile & Desktop web

Impact

120% increase in car rental bookings

The modernized View Reservation page. The starting point for every post-booking traveler action.

The Problem

A decade of patchwork on a page that couldn't afford to break

Alaska was preparing for the biggest expansion in its history (the acquisition of Hawaiian Airlines, going global) on top of an online reservation system still running on "the monolith." Innocent code changes there caused outages in places no one expected.

View Reservation, the single most important post-booking page for every traveler, hadn't been meaningfully updated in over a decade. Instead of redesigning, teams had layered alert banners on alert banners: RealID, pandemic protocols, policy changes. Travelers hit a wall of mixed-era, mixed-relevance notifications before they could find their flight.

Fun fact: Alaska was the first airline to sell tickets online. The age of some of these systems reflected that pioneering spirit, for better and worse.

The team had also struggled with retention. PMs, engineers, and designers had cycled through, frustrated by the constraints. No one had stayed long enough to push through a real overhaul.

Kick Off

A two-day workshop to get everyone in the same room

I ran a cross-functional workshop at Alaska's Seattle headquarters with design, product, and engineering. Before flying in, every attendee used the live product on their flight. Phone, tablet, desktop, screenshots of every friction point. Everyone arrived with first-hand evidence.

Together we audited the page and surfaced problems no one had named:

  • No built-in analytics. We were flying blind on user behavior.
  • The page wasn't responsive. At all.
  • Alaska's most important revenue opportunity (the credit card offer) was buried below a wall of ads.
  • Years of regulatory changes left outdated and critical information jumbled with no hierarchy.
  • No menu of actions. Guests looking for something as simple as flight notifications had to hunt.
  • Subpages had no consistent design patterns or brand coherence.

Scope was bigger than anyone anticipated. We left Seattle with four months of intense design work ahead.

Research

Heatmaps, interviews, and hard truths

Partnering with Alaska's UX research team, I worked through heatmaps and early-stage interviews. The findings shaped every design decision that followed.

  • Users scanned the page multiple times to find where to enter TSA information.
  • Guests who had already checked in were checking in again. The button didn't reflect their status.
  • Only 16% of users scrolled past a block of ads near the bottom, missing valuable trip information.
  • Users tolerated data collection as long as it improved their experience.
  • Travelers got frustrated when airlines showed ads before flight details. They wanted their information first.

Challenges

Losing the PM, stretching the design system, scaling under pressure

Shortly after the workshop, our PM left. I absorbed the role. Leadership shakeouts, roadmap meetings, the bridge between our team and the business, all while keeping design work on track. My design manager and PM leadership backed me up, but day-to-day was mine to run.

Alaska's Auro design system was still young, with components built for the revenue side of the business (ticket sales). We adapted what we needed for the post-booking surface without overburdening the design system team, essentially extending Auro as we used it.

Meanwhile, the app version of View Reservation (built by a vendor) hit engineering issues, driving even more traffic to web and raising the stakes on our timeline.

Design & Rollout

Shipping incrementally, learning continuously

After three months of iteration, we shipped an MVP that addressed the highest-stakes issues: a personalization layer, a side menu for page actions, built-in analytics, consistent notification patterns, and thoughtfully placed ads and upsells.

Rollout went to progressively larger user groups: one-way single passengers, then multi-passenger, then round trips, then mixed-carrier reservations. As each group came in, we monitored and adjusted.

The drop in customer care calls was so steep we replaced the 800 number on the modernized page with text and chat links only.

Honest Reflection

What didn't work

The initial design featured a large hero image of the trip destination, taking up three-quarters of the mobile screen. It tested well in research. After launch, real users found it obnoxious, especially those traveling for serious reasons. I reduced it to about one-fifth of the screen so flight details were visible without scrolling.

I also experimented with reordering page content based on proximity to the trip (passenger info two weeks out, flight details 72 hours before departure). It disoriented users who expected a consistent layout. We pulled the feature with plans to reintroduce it later, paired with onboarding that explained the personalization.
